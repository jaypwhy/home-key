Phase 0 — framework, not a promise

Case studies — seven ways to live

Every situation is different, so homes are built around the people in them — never the other way around. These seven walkthroughs show how one system meets seven ways of living: six home types, each carrying both halves of the same duality, and one that is a way of living in itself.

Where this stands today (Phase 0)

0 people housed. No funds raised. No entity formed. No partner committed. Nothing on this page is a promise, a listing, or an offer of housing, and none of it is a schedule anyone is living on. Every case study below is an illustrative framework walkthrough — no real household is described, no invented person or outcome story appears anywhere, and no cost is quoted here. Where a figure does appear, it is either the pilot’s full-vision framework band (labeled as such) or a verified San Diego market reference with its check date.

How to read a case study

A home in this model arrives two ways. In one, it is already renovated and you arrive to it finished. In the other, you move in and it becomes around you — the Living-in-Development / Living-in-Renovation track, where vacant and underused space is turned into a home in a fixed order. Both are real homes, and neither is the lesser one. The third dimension, sensory-friendly spaces, spans both: it is not a stage a home reaches, it is a standard the home holds from the first night.

The order the work happens in

  1. Secure and safe — locks, detectors, safety mods, flooring stabilized, low-VOC paint, lighting, Wi-Fi.
  2. Wet rooms — kitchen and bath to code and finished. Kitchen function unlocks food autonomy.
  3. Sleep zones — bedrooms ready, quiet, dark-capable.
  4. Work zones — a work base with connectivity live, for remote-first roles.
  5. Comfort and decor — the calm space finished as its own work, along with the rest of the home.

An inspection checkpoint sits between each phase. A miss is a punch item, not a verdict on the household — it is fixed and the check is run again.

What renovation really costs — the one verified anchor

Verified San Diego market reference: moderate rehab runs about $50–$75 per square foot (checked 2026-09-06), so a modest 1,000-square-foot rehab is roughly $50,000–$75,000 in contractor terms. This is a market benchmark, not a program figure and not a price for any home described here; it is why the renovation track is real work at real cost, and why the budget is padded rather than guessed. For scale reference, the same verification put the San Diego fair market rent at $2,062 for a studio and $2,248 for a one-bedroom (checked 2026-09-06) — again a market reference, not a promise about anyone’s home.

The pilot’s own budget bands live on the pilot proposal page; they are full-vision framework figures, not commitments, and they are not re-tabulated here.

Case study 1 · Apartment

One home behind one door in a larger building — the most common shape of a soft landing, and the easiest to find in a city.

Duality state 1 of 2 · the finished home

Already renovated — ready the day you arrive

  • In this home: the unit behind its own door is the whole build — every spec, punch list item and appliance belongs to the household alone, and the building around it stays as it is.
  • The calm space is an interior-room nook set away from the entry and corridor, so door noise and foot traffic are not in the room the household goes to for quiet.

Duality state 2 of 2 · the home becoming

Living in development / living in renovation — you move in, and it becomes around you

  • In this home: the work lives inside the unit, and the boundary is the unit’s own wall. Anything the building shares is coordinated around the household rather than run through it.
  • The calm-space nook is carved and separated in Phase 1 — off the entry path, away from where the work enters — so the room a person goes to for quiet is never the room with the ladder in it.

Sensory-friendly spaces in this home

This is not a stage a home reaches — it is a standard that holds in both states, at every phase, from the first night.

In the already-renovated home

Where the calm space lives here: An interior-room nook, off the entry path and away from door and corridor noise — separated by a full door where a room allows it, or a ceiling-height curtain where it is a nook.

  • No motion-only lighting in the calm or sleep zones — dimmable warm-white LED on a predictable physical switch, flicker-free at every dim step (AUT-6, AUT-7).
  • Quiet-close hardware on kitchen and bath cabinets and drawers, spec’d at install (AUT-8).
  • Same-place-everything built-ins at fixed homes, not loose baskets: labeled pantry bins, entry bench and hooks, a charger station, a meds station — each with a live outlet at the station so no cord crosses a room (AUT-4, AUT-17).
  • Sensory-friendly textures: washable low-pile rugs with pads and no shag or high-pile in circulation paths (AUT-13), tagless/soft bedding stocked in two or three pre-vetted textures the person picks and may switch once (AUT-11), scratch-resistant low-sensory floors and low-VOC paint throughout (AUT-14).
  • Predictable layout: room roles stay put, and the calm space keeps its own switch, its seating and its sightline to the room rather than being re-decided at move-in (AUT-1, AUT-2).
  • Routine infrastructure that does not move: a mounted daily-flow card at the fridge, a mounted whiteboard calendar at the entry, and utilities, internet and cleaning on autopay set up with the person at move-in and changeable by them at any time (AUT-15, AUT-16, AUT-18).

In the living-in-development / living-in-renovation home

How the quiet holds here: The nook’s shell, separation and dimmable switch go in with Phase 1 secure-and-safe work, so the quietest room in the home is never the last one on the list.

  • The calm zone is never sacrificed to construction. It is built first or protected throughout: its shell and separability start in Phase 1 so the space exists from the first night, and its finishing is its own Phase-5 work (AUT-1, AUT-2).
  • Loud work is scheduled noise-aware — around the household’s sleep, work and decompression times — and decompression time itself is protected rather than scheduled over (AUT-5).
  • Acoustic basics land before the space is used: door sweep, rug pads, wall panels in the calm space (AUT-3, AUT-9).
  • A dust-free sanctuary holds for the whole renovation: the work stays on the far side of a sealed or curtained boundary, and unscented laundry and cleaning basics are stocked in the unit rather than brought in from the site (AUT-12).
  • The lighting rule holds during the work too — no motion-only control, no harsh overhead fluorescent into the calm or sleep zone, dimming on a physical switch a person can find in the dark (AUT-6, AUT-7).
  • Boundaries are checked, not assumed: the calm space’s door or full-height curtain separates, the switch is findable in the dark, and nothing has been booked on top of the space.

Who fits here: One person, a couple, or a small household. Unit sizes in the type run from studio to multi-bedroom, and the household’s own composition decides which — through the program’s household-and-family framework and its unit-size ladder, pointed to here and not re-tabulated on this page.

Case study 7 · Living in development / living in renovation — the way of living itself

The seventh case is not a building type. It is the track itself: vacant or underused space — an empty office, a rundown hotel, a surplus property — becoming a home while somebody lives in it. It is a real housing state, and the frame is glass-half-full throughout: a door, a key, and a home that improves around you.

What this way of living actually is

  • A key and a door on day one

    The home starts the day the person moves in — no rent, no eviction clock, and no waiting in a queue for the last coat of paint. Secure and safe comes first, so the first night is safe by definition, and the wet rooms follow so cooking and showering unassisted arrive early.

  • The quiet room is never last

    The decompression space is either finished first or protected throughout, so the room a person goes to for quiet exists from the beginning — not after the house is pretty.

  • Rooms come online one at a time

    Each finished space is a finished space, usable and held to spec, with an inspection checkpoint behind it. The home grows a room at a time instead of arriving all at once or not at all.

  • The home improves around the person

    The work adapts to the household — schedule, boundaries, placements, textures — rather than the household adapting to the work. Where a spec and a person disagree, the person wins and the build changes (AUT-F1 – AUT-F4).

The space was already here, standing empty. The work of this track is turning what exists into a home — and that is why living through it counts as a way to live, not a way to wait.

Who does the work

The build as written: licensed contractors

In the model as written, the work is contractor work — licensed trades, real inspections, real sign-offs. So that no one can misread it: this is not a household renovating in exchange for housing, and housing is never traded for labor.

Under consideration — not decided

Whether participants would also work on the build — as a paid work-crew with a training pathway — is an option still under consideration, recorded as an open question in the program’s own operating notes. It is not decided, not scheduled, and not on this page as a promise. If it is ever adopted, the design intent is already written down: paid work at a living wage, kept structurally separate from housing, because the two must never be the same transaction — no one works for their home, and no home depends on anyone’s work.

The honest half, said plainly

What is guaranteed in this state

  • Secure and safe before the first night, verified with the person walking the punch list.
  • Kitchen and bath next, so daily life works early rather than late.
  • A protected, sensory-friendly calm space from the beginning.
  • Cleaning and connectivity as live rights, from day one.
  • No rent, no eviction clock, and no leverage over the household through the work.

What is deliberately open

  • Some people would rather not live through a renovation at all — which is exactly why the already-renovated path is the other half of this duality. The household chooses; nobody is assigned to a construction site.
  • Timelines depend on trades, permits and materials, so no dates are promised here.
  • Whether participant work-crews ever exist is undecided (see above), and no one’s housing is waiting on that answer.
  • Every cost figure in this track is a framework estimate, and the only real number in this file is the verified ~$50–$75 per square foot San Diego moderate rehab reference (checked 2026-09-06).

Who fits here: any household size the unit-size ladder and bedroom-based scaling allow, decided by the household through the program’s household-and-family framework — pointed to here, never re-tabulated. Where a household has a live safety plan, layout and safety come before size, and the sequence flexes around safety rather than the reverse.
